For years, the kratom industry has had an answer whenever lawmakers considered prohibition:

Pass a Kratom Consumer Protection Act.

Require labels.

That sales pitch just got considerably more expensive.

On August 26, 2026, the Drug Enforcement Administration's temporary scheduling order for mitragynine pseudoindoxyl (MP), MGM-15 and MGM-16 takes effect.

These aren't new labeling violations.

They aren't ingredients DEA merely recommends manufacturers avoid.

They are Schedule I controlled substances.

And DEA could hardly have made the consequence clearer:

"Retail sales of schedule I controlled substances to the general public are not allowed under the CSA."

Then comes the part every state legislator considering another kratom regulatory scheme should read:

"Possession of any quantity of mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15, or MGM-16 … is unlawful."

Any quantity.

So now we'd like to ask the states keeping kratom legal a very simple question:

Who is testing the kratom?

Because Apparently the Label Isn't Going to Tell You

This would be an easier problem if MP products always came in a package screaming:

WARNING: THIS PRODUCT CONTAINS MITRAGYNINE PSEUDOINDOXYL.

Unfortunately for regulators, scientists have already gone shopping.

And what they found should make every legislator who was promised that kratom could simply be "regulated responsibly" start asking questions.

A 2026 study in the Journal of the American Society for Mass Spectrometry examined authentic kratom material and commercial kratom products using sophisticated mass-spectrometry analysis.

The researchers found an important distinction.

They did not confirm MP in authenticated kratom leaf.

Good.

Now here's the other half.

They confirmed MP in:

  • 8 of 9 tablets
  • and 2 capsules.

And how many products were actually labeled as containing MP?

One.

MP was detected in seven additional tablet products and two capsule products where it wasn't declared.

So much for solving this with labels.

"But Our Kratom Is Lab Tested"

Another 2026 investigation analyzed commercial kratom products and examined the chemical composition of modern 7-OH products and the formation of mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.

It is a marketplace containing products with wildly different alkaloid profiles, different degrees of processing and, in some cases, compounds that look nothing like what legislators probably imagine when somebody walks into a hearing carrying a picture of a kratom tree.

Congratulations. Your Kratom Law Now Needs a Chemistry Department.

And not just any test will do.

The 2026 mass-spectrometry study found that kratom's chemistry creates an analytical problem.

Several structurally related compounds can produce overlapping signals.

The researchers demonstrated that inadequate analytical criteria can result in false identification. Reliable confirmation requires appropriate chromatographic separation and diagnostic mass-spectrometry evidence.

Even DEA acknowledges that these closely related compounds require specific methods and instrumentation for accurate identification and notes the challenges forensic laboratories face obtaining standards and validating methods.

So when somebody tells a legislature:

"Don't worry. We'll require lab testing."

Ask the next question.

What lab testing?

Then keep asking.

  • What method?
  • What detection limit?
  • What reference standard?
  • Who validates the laboratory?
  • Who collects the sample?
  • Who verifies the lot?
  • Who makes sure the bottle tested is actually representative of the 5,000 bottles shipped into the state?
  • Who conducts random retail testing?
  • Who pays for it?
  • Who recalls the product?
  • Who identifies every retailer carrying the failed lot?
  • Who takes possession of the inventory after it tests positive for a Schedule I substance?
  • And how often are you going to repeat all of this?

Because the next truckload arrives tomorrow.

A QR Code Is Not Drug Enforcement

This is where the "regulate, don't ban" slogan runs into reality.

A QR code isn't chemical analysis.

A manufacturer's promise isn't chemical analysis.

A pretty certificate uploaded to a website isn't independent state surveillance.

And a label saying "natural kratom" doesn't magically remove a controlled substance from the package.

If states insist on maintaining a legal kratom market, they need a system capable of distinguishing products that may legally be sold from products containing federally Schedule I substances.

That means real testing.

Real laboratories.

Real enforcement.

And real money.

All so the corner vape shop can continue selling kratom.

It Gets Better: Kratom Chemistry Doesn't Sit Still

There is another wrinkle.

The World Health Organization's scientific review describes mitragynine converting into 7-hydroxymitragynine under room temperature and sunlight without an added reagent. WHO specifically considered it plausible that this could occur in dried leaf exposed to air for prolonged periods.

WHO also describes MP as a major transformation product when a fungus acts upon mitragynine.

To be clear—and unlike some people in this debate, we're interested in getting the chemistry right—WHO also states that MP is not found naturally in the kratom plant itself.

So no, DEA's order does not magically turn every kratom leaf in America into Schedule I contraband.

That's not the argument.

The argument is worse for the industry's regulatory pitch:

You cannot determine the legal status of every finished commercial "kratom" product merely by looking at it.

You have to know what's actually inside.

And now one of the things you have to look for is a Schedule I controlled substance.

DEA Just Changed the Equation

DEA didn't ban ordinary kratom through this action.

It did something that should nevertheless fundamentally change the way state lawmakers think about regulating the kratom marketplace.

It put mitragynine pseudoindoxyl into Schedule I.

Researchers have already detected MP in commercial products.

Researchers have already found it in products that didn't disclose it.

Certificates of analysis for named kratom products report measurable MP.

Scientists have already warned that accurately identifying it requires appropriate analytical methods.

And DEA says unauthorized possession of:

ANY QUANTITY

is unlawful.
