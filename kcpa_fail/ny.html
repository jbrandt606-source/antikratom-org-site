New York Passed a Law to Protect Minors. Apparently, Nobody Told the Smoke Shops.

A 20-year-old reporter, 18 smoke shops and the difference between passing a law and enforcing one.

New York celebrated its new kratom age restriction as a landmark public-health measure. On October 10, 2026, the New York Post published the results of an undercover test: a 20-year-old reporter visited 18 Manhattan smoke shops, and only nine asked for identification. A law written in Albany is of little use to a teenager if the retailer never asks her age.

The numbers Albany should answer for

18 shops visited. The reporter tested stores along Eighth Avenue in Hell’s Kitchen.

9 requested ID. The other nine did not request identification, according to the Post.

2 asked her age, but not for ID. Asking a customer whether she is 21 is not the same as verifying it.

$500. The age-restriction legislation provides a civil penalty of up to $500 for a violation.

Important distinction: The Post’s findings about offers and ID checks should not be described as 18 completed illegal sales, or as proof that every shop committed a statutory violation.

1. The Law Was Supposed to Protect People Under 21

In December 2025, Governor Kathy Hochul announced legislation prohibiting kratom sales to people under 21. Her office described the measures as landmark legislation designed to protect New Yorkers from kratom-related harm. The age restriction was enacted through S4552A/A2340A, followed by chapter amendments in S8814/A9472, signed in February 2026.

There was nothing obscure about the promise. New York would establish an age floor and attach civil penalties to violations. The harder part was always going to be making a retail counter obey it.

Albany passed the age restriction. The New York Post went to see whether anyone was checking ages.

2. Meet the Lawmakers Behind the Promise

Senator Patricia Fahy sponsored S4552A, the enacted Senate age-restriction bill. Assemblymember John T. McDonald III sponsored its Assembly companion, A2340A. Senator James Tedisco co-sponsored S4552A. Governor Kathy Hochul signed the bill. The Senate approved it 59–0 in June 2025.

These lawmakers deserve accurate credit for passing the restriction. They also deserve the obvious follow-up: after the press releases, what was done to make sure retailers actually checked identification?

Behold New York’s Kratom Regulation.

Albany held the hearings, passed the bill, signed the law and celebrated protecting young people. The smoke shops were apparently left to decide whether the law applied to them.

The legislature built a barrier. The industry got a sidewalk.

3. Nine Shops Did Not Ask for ID

The Post sent 20-year-old reporter Sidney Blasco into 18 smoke shops in Hell’s Kitchen. Nine asked for identification. Two of the others reportedly asked whether she was 21 without requiring ID. The reporting documents a striking inconsistency in retail age screening, though the precise legal conclusion for each store depends on what was offered or sold and the applicable statutory requirements.

New York did not discover an obscure chemistry loophole here. It encountered the oldest problem in retail enforcement: a rule that exists on paper but is not consistently observed at the counter.

4. A $500 Penalty Is Not an Enforcement Strategy

Under the age-restriction law, selling or providing kratom to someone under 21 can carry a civil penalty of no more than $500. That number alone does not establish whether enforcement is effective. Inspection frequency, repeat violations, retailer education and actual penalties matter at least as much.

Before lawmakers congratulate themselves again, they should publish the numbers: how many inspections were conducted, how many violations were confirmed, how many penalties were assessed and how many retailers repeatedly failed compliance checks.

A press release is not an ID scanner. A bill signing is not an inspection.

5. The Broader Problem Does Not Disappear at Age 21

Age restrictions are not meaningless. They establish a legal boundary and can reduce access when enforced. But New York’s law does not make kratom products safe for adults, resolve questions about potency or dependence, or establish that every product on a smoke-shop shelf is lawful.

The Post also published a separate investigation into shops selling products reported to contain prohibited kratom-related compounds. That is a distinct investigation, not proof that the age-restriction law itself caused those sales. Together, the reports raise a question about whether the state’s retail oversight is keeping pace with the market.

6. What New York Owes Its Residents

The legislators who backed the age restriction should request public compliance data, identify the agency responsible for inspections and enforcement, and explain how repeat violators are handled. The state should also clarify how it investigates mislabeled products and suspected controlled compounds.

New York can argue about the merits of a full kratom prohibition. What it cannot credibly do is mistake enactment for enforcement.

They called it landmark protection. Nine of eighteen shops did not even ask the reporter for ID.
