South Carolina Had a 2% 7-OH Limit. The Senate Took It Out.

A 2025 KCPA, a 2026 Schedule I bill rewritten through amendments, and a family asking what “consumer protection” actually meant.

South Carolina lawmakers have spent two years drawing lines around kratom: natural or synthetic, two percent or twenty milligrams, leaf or extract, isolated or concentrated. The record shows exactly how those lines moved.

A Family Is Asking What “Consumer Protection” Meant

FOX Carolina reported September 22, 2026 that Robert and Dana Knox are mourning their 29-year-old son, Micah. His parents said he had been sober for nine and a half months. The report says toxicology found mitragynine in his blood and describes his death as an accidental overdose.

Robert Knox told FOX Carolina: “He didn’t deserve to die from something that he bought at a gas station.”

The report does not establish that 7-OH itself caused Micah's death. It does raise a direct question for a state that already enacted a law called the Kratom Consumer Protection Act: what did the law actually prohibit, and how could regulators verify compliance?

The 2% Limit Was There. Then It Was Gone.

The April 15, 2025 version of S.221 prohibited a product containing more than 2% 7-hydroxymitragynine in the alkaloid fraction. The Senate Medical Affairs Committee later proposed an amendment deleting that numerical ceiling and narrowing the synthetic language to “fully synthetic” alkaloids. Sen. Penry Gustafson Garrett explained the committee amendment on the floor. The Senate adopted it and passed the bill 39-0. Gov. Henry McMaster signed S.221 on May 12, 2025.

The enacted KCPA requires labels to disclose the amount of mitragynine and 7-OH, but contains no 2% ceiling. Its synthetic prohibition applies to a fully synthetic alkaloid.

A KCPA Is Only as Strong as Its Enforcement

A clerk or ordinary retail inspector cannot determine by sight whether a tablet contains 1.9% or 2.1% 7-OH, whether a labeled milligram amount is accurate, whether isolated 7-OH was added, or how an alkaloid was manufactured. Those questions require testing, traceability and—where the law turns on manufacturing origin—evidence about the production process.

That is the practical problem with increasingly intricate chemical carve-outs. Every percentage, milligram ceiling and synthetic-versus-natural distinction creates another fact the state must independently verify.

Then H.4641 Started as a Schedule I Bill

H.4641 was introduced to add kratom to Schedule I and repeal the KCPA. The House Judiciary Committee substituted a regulatory approach. Rep. William Bailey then offered an amendment restoring broad Schedule I treatment and repeal. Rep. James Rutherford moved to table it; the motion succeeded 54-45.

On April 29, the House considered additional amendments. Rep. Case Brittain moved to table proposals addressing synthetic compounds and 7-OH and moved to table Rep. John McCravy's broad Schedule I/repeal proposal. Brittain also offered a detailed KCPA rewrite; it was tabled. Rutherford offered another KCPA rewrite with a 20 mg 7-OH ceiling and retail-location restrictions; it too was tabled.

The Federal Landscape Has Changed Too

On July 1, 2026, DEA announced that it had filed notices of intent to temporarily place high-concentration 7-OH and the related substances mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15 and MGM-16 into Schedule I. DEA said the restrictions apply once temporary scheduling orders take effect. That distinction matters: a notice of intent is not the same thing as saying every listed substance was already federally scheduled on July 1.

The Question South Carolina Still Has to Answer

South Carolina's record shows multiple competing approaches: a numerical 7-OH cap, a “fully synthetic” distinction, broad Schedule I treatment, a 2% KCPA rewrite, a 20 mg proposal, certificates of analysis and retail-location restrictions. Those are materially different systems.

The enforcement question is therefore unavoidable: who tests the product, how often, against which validated method, with what lot traceability, and how does the state establish whether the chemistry and manufacturing pathway match the label?

Micah Knox was a person, not a statutory threshold. His family's story puts the consequences of that policy debate outside the State House and inside an ordinary retail market.
