Utah · KCPA Fail · Testing

Four Deaths. Then They Tested the Products.

Utah regulated kratom. Officials later reported Schedule I kratom-derived compounds in products labeled as cat's claw. The label did not announce what the laboratory found.

Utah officials say four deaths have been linked to potent semi-synthetic kratom compounds. Testing found mitragynine pseudoindoxyl and MGM-15 in products sold as “cat's claw.” The important part is the order: people died, products were tested, and then the chemistry became visible.

The 30-second version

Utah had rules. MP and MGM-15 were illegal in the state and were recently placed in Schedule I by DEA.

The products did not have to confess. Officials said products were misleadingly labeled as cat's claw and could claim to be legal and free of kratom.

Testing found something else. Utah officials reported MP and MGM-15.

Four deaths were linked to potent semi-synthetic kratom compounds. One death was linked by Utah Poison Control's medical director to a Buzzers product.

Before the Deaths: Utah Was the KCPA Experiment

Utah was not late to kratom regulation. It was first. In 2019, Utah became the first state to enact the industry-backed Kratom Consumer Protection Act. The law kept kratom legal while relying on product restrictions, testing, registration and labeling requirements. It even prohibited controlled substances and required labels to disclose mitragynine and 7-OH.

Sen. Mike McKell voted for that framework while serving in the House. By 2026, he was publicly calling that vote a mistake. He introduced S.B. 45, originally pursuing a broader ban, and ultimately carried the legislation that repealed Utah's original KCPA and replaced it with a far narrower market: only “pure leaf kratom” may now be sold in Utah, extracts and products made from extracts are prohibited from sale, and retail sales are limited to registered tobacco specialty businesses.

Utah helped invent the KCPA model in 2019. Seven years later, one of the lawmakers who voted for it helped repeal it.

1. People Died. Then Came the Testing.

This is the part every legislature writing another Kratom Consumer Protection Act should read twice.

FirstProducts reached consumers.
ThenDeaths drew attention.
ThenTesting identified the chemistry.

Utah officials warned that products labeled as cat's claw contained mitragynine pseudoindoxyl and MGM-15. Both were illegal in Utah and had recently been classified by DEA as Schedule I.

The law knew the compounds were illegal. The package did not have to tell anyone they were inside.

2. The Ridiculous Part: The Law Still Depends on the Seller Telling the Truth

Officials said products were misleadingly labeled as cat's claw, Uncaria tomentosa. The labels reportedly claimed the products were “legal and free of kratom” while warning that they were for adults or produced strong psychoactive effects.

Laboratory testing reportedly found kratom-derived Schedule I compounds instead.

A package can say “cat's claw,” “botanical extract” or “proprietary blend.” A controlled substance does not become visible because lawmakers wrote its name into a statute.

3. Your Bill Can Ban It. Now Find It.

A statute can prohibit MP. It can prohibit MGM-15. It can prohibit the next analog lawmakers learn about. None of that tells an inspector what is inside a tablet.

The cashier cannot identify it. The package may not disclose it. A visual inspection cannot identify it. The state needs analytical testing capable of detecting the compound.

Questions every KCPA sponsor should answer

Who tests? Which agency routinely buys these products and screens them?

Test for what? Does the laboratory have methods and reference material for every prohibited analog?

How often? Is testing systematic, or does it begin after poison-center calls, overdoses or deaths?

Who pays? A list of prohibited molecules is cheap to print. A serious surveillance program is not.

4. The Manufacturer Said It Wasn't Kratom

NBC News reported that Exo Club, which makes Buzzers, said on its website that the product does not contain kratom and described extracting an indole alkaloid from cat's claw to mirror the effects of popular kratom products.

Utah officials reported something different from testing: kratom-derived MP and MGM-15 in products represented as cat's claw.

The website can make a claim. The mass spectrometer gets the last word.

5. The Supply Chain Does Not End at the Leaf

6. “It's Illegal” Is Not a Testing Program

If nobody is routinely testing products, a prohibited compound can remain invisible to the state until something gives officials a reason to look.

In this case, officials are discussing four deaths linked to potent semi-synthetic kratom compounds.

Four deaths. Schedule I compounds. Products labeled as another plant.

Utah did not need another ingredient list. It needed to know what was actually in the package.

The Question for Every State With a KCPA

Do not tell the public that your bill “covers” MP, MGM-15 or some future analog and call the job finished.

Explain how your state will find it when the manufacturer does not put the name on the package. Explain which laboratory will test it. Explain how frequently products will be screened.

And explain why the testing should happen before somebody dies.

“The label says it isn't there” is not a laboratory result.

“The statute says it's illegal” is not an analytical method.
