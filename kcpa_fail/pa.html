Pennsylvania HB 2657 Draws a Chemical Line. Who Verifies What Is in the Bag?

Rep. Bridget Kosierowski’s bill would keep “natural” kratom legal, prohibit products that cross defined chemical thresholds, register sellers and require labels. The practical question is how those distinctions are verified at retail.

HB 2657 is sponsored by Rep. Bridget Kosierowski. It would create Pennsylvania’s Regulation and Oversight of Kratom Act and preserve retail sales of products the bill defines as natural-form kratom.

The 30-second version

Allowed: dried, cut-and-sifted leaf or raw leaf powder that meets the bill’s “natural kratom product” definition.

Prohibited: products the bill defines as synthetic, including those above 0.5% 7-hydroxymitragynine in the alkaloid fraction or above 10 ppm mitragynine pseudoindoxyl.

Required: processor and retailer registration, 21+ sales, child-resistant packaging and specified label statements.

Not specified in the bill text: a mandatory premarket certificate of analysis for every lot or a retail field test that independently verifies the package contents.

1. The Bill Depends on Knowing What Is Actually in the Product

HB 2657 defines “natural” and “synthetic” kratom by product form and chemistry. That means enforcement ultimately turns on composition—not simply what the front of a package says.

The bill requires a label to identify the processor, state that FDA has not evaluated the product, verify that the product is in natural form, and warn consumers to keep it away from minors. But the introduced text does not expressly require every retail lot to carry an independent laboratory certificate proving those chemical limits before sale.

HB 2657’s proposed cutoff is 10 ppm mitragynine pseudoindoxyl. The cited OPMS Silver sample measured 1.34 mg/g—equivalent to about 1,340 ppm.

2. Federal Law Changed the Baseline

On August 26, 2026, DEA temporarily placed mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, MGM-15 and MGM-16 in federal Schedule I. DEA states that unauthorized possession, manufacture, distribution and sale of those substances are subject to the Controlled Substances Act.

That creates an important distinction for Pennsylvania lawmakers: HB 2657 uses a state-law definition that calls a product “synthetic” when mitragynine pseudoindoxyl exceeds 10 ppm, while federal Schedule I control applies to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl itself, subject to federal law and its authorized exceptions.

3. What HB 2657 Would Actually Do

Processors and retailers would register annually with the Department of Health. Retailers could sell qualifying natural-form kratom only to adults 21 and older and would have to use an electronic age-verification device. Packaging would have to be resealable and child-resistant.

The bill also prohibits marketing aimed at people under 21, medical claims, and packaging that resembles commercially sold food, candy or beverages. Knowing or intentional violations involving synthetic kratom could carry criminal penalties; violations of the retail rules could carry civil penalties.

4. The Enforcement Questions

The bill gives the Department of Health registration and enforcement responsibilities and funds enforcement with registration fees. The text, however, leaves practical questions for implementation: Who samples products from shelves? Which laboratory method is used? How often are lots tested? Who pays? How quickly can a product be removed while testing is pending? And how does a retailer independently know that a supplier’s “natural form” verification is chemically accurate?

The core policy question is straightforward: if legality depends on chemistry, Pennsylvania will need a reliable way to verify the chemistry.
