Bad Science. Real Trash.

Seven polished position papers. Green headers. Charts. Footnotes. Lots of scientific vocabulary.

And one very consistent conclusion:

Whatever the problem is, apparently the answer can never be maybe we shouldn't be selling this stuff.

KCAC wants legislators to believe virtually every problem in the kratom market can be repaired with another label, another registration form, another laboratory test, another age restriction, or another government inspector.

The interesting part is how much KCAC admits while making that argument.

Its own papers describe dependence, addiction concerns, drug interactions, heavy metals, pediatric exposures, unlabeled products, unregistered products and an enforcement system that vendors routinely ignore.

Then the papers somehow arrive at:

Keep selling it. Just regulate it better.

That is not the only conclusion the evidence permits.

This isn't peer review. It's advocacy with footnotes.

Seventeen pages later, heroin is still worse than kratom. We checked.

1. The Eight-Factor Analysis

The centerpiece. The conclusion appears before the analysis even starts.

This is the centerpiece.

The conclusion appears before the analysis even starts:

"Blanket Bans Could Worsen Public Health."

KCAC also identifies itself as an independent council while acknowledging that the Global Kratom Coalition established it.

That does not automatically make the analysis wrong.

It does tell legislators what they are reading: an advocacy position paper supporting a particular policy outcome.

Much of the argument then becomes comparative:

  • Kratom versus heroin.
  • Kratom versus fentanyl.
  • Kratom versus illicit opioids.

That makes kratom look pretty good.

So would comparing warm beer to antifreeze.

The legislative question is not: Is kratom safer than heroin?

The question is: Has kratom itself been shown safe enough, consistent enough and controllable enough to justify continued retail sale?

Those are very different questions.

KCAC also cites surveys where kratom users describe kratom as beneficial, therapeutic or even lifesaving.

Those experiences matter.

But people saying a product helped them is not the same thing as proving that product is an effective medical treatment.

And buried in the same analysis is the admission that natural kratom can be abused and that addiction can harm patients.

"Not as bad as heroin" is an impressively low standard for something sold next to energy drinks.

2. The Evidence Standard That Moves Around

This may be the most important theme on the entire page.

This may be the most important theme on the entire page.

KCAC spends considerable effort explaining that stronger human studies deserve more confidence than animal studies, case reports and weaker observational evidence. Its 7-OH paper explicitly presents that evidence hierarchy.

Good.

Then KCAC makes an extraordinary admission:

Neither kratom nor 7-OH has clinical trials in patients with a disease proving that the products work or fully defining their risks.

Read that again.

No clinical trials showing kratom treats a disease.

And yet KCAC repeatedly uses weaker evidence to discuss supposed benefits, opioid substitution and favorable risk.

That produces a remarkably convenient rule:

  • Weak evidence against kratom? Not good enough. We need better studies.
  • Weak evidence supporting kratom? Interesting. Let's tell the legislature.
That isn't a hierarchy of evidence.

That's an elevator.

3. "Benefits Due to 7-OH"

This paper lectures others about being careful with weak evidence. Then it goes right back to animal studies.

This paper lectures others about being careful with weak evidence.

Then it goes right back to animal studies.

It also makes several important admissions.

KCAC acknowledges that mitragynine is converted into 7-OH inside the body. It further describes 7-OH as leading to mitragynine pseudoindoxyl, another opioid-active compound.

So the honest discussion is not:

"Does oral kratom have anything to do with 7-OH?"

It plainly does.

The question is how much the downstream compounds contribute to effects and risk.

That is a legitimate scientific debate.

Pretending the relationship disappears because concentrated 7-OH products are worse is not.

KCAC itself also acknowledges that it lacks enough evidence to make definitive claims that kratom treats severe pain or opioid-use disorder.

KCAC wants enormous caution before accepting evidence of harm — and substantially less caution before discussing benefit.

4. "Non-Swallowed Formulations"

KCAC says vaping, sublingual, nasal, transdermal, injectable and other non-swallowed kratom products should not be sold.

KCAC says vaping, sublingual, nasal, transdermal, injectable and other non-swallowed kratom products should not be sold.

Why? Because bypassing normal digestion can produce higher blood levels and increase risk.

Makes sense.

Then the paper calls powders, capsules, tablets and liquids "acceptable" when properly manufactured and labeled.

That is where the logic jumps the curb.

Showing that one route can be more dangerous does not establish that another route is safe.

Administration route matters. Apparently so does the route taken to reach the conclusion.
"Please swallow it instead" is not a clinical safety determination.

5. "Kratom Use in Children"

This paper may be my favorite accidental argument for stricter control.

This paper may be my favorite accidental argument for stricter control.

KCAC acknowledges pediatric kratom exposures involving: agitation, hallucinations, seizures, rapid heart rate and nausea.

It says children may be especially vulnerable to addiction and notes concerns about excessive lead exposure from some kratom products.

Then KCAC recommends:

  • 21+ sales.
  • Age verification.
  • Habit-forming warnings.
  • No cartoon mascots.
  • No candy-like formulations.
  • No self-service access.
Just your ordinary natural wellness product requiring tobacco-style age controls.

KCAC is absolutely right that children should be protected.

But the paper raises an obvious question:

What exactly are lawmakers protecting them from?

Because the answer in KCAC's own paper is not "nothing."

If it needs ID checks, addiction warnings and rules against disguising it as candy, maybe stop selling it like candy-adjacent wellness merchandise.

6. "Proper Labeling"

This paper is less a defense of the market than an indictment of it.

This paper is less a defense of the market than an indictment of it.

KCAC says it is aware of products being sold:

  • without meaningful labels;
  • without serving sizes;
  • without reliable ways to measure liquid doses;
  • with increasingly concentrated alkaloid delivery;
  • without habit-forming warnings;
  • with possible heavy-metal problems;
  • and with possible medication interactions.

That is quite a list.

And the proposed cure? A better label.

KCAC even proposes labels telling consumers a recommended amount and the number of servings that can "safely" be consumed in 24 hours.

Okay.

Who established the safe number?

Because printing "two servings per day" on a bottle does not turn two servings per day into a clinically established safe dose.

Step 1: Determine the safe dose. Step 2: Put it on the label. Skipping Step 1 seems important.
Labels communicate evidence. They do not manufacture evidence.

7. "Registration and Enforcement"

This one barely needs commentary.

This one barely needs commentary.

KCAC says product and vendor registration can help protect consumers.

Then it describes an audit of 28 Utah vendors.

The results?

  • 85.7% were selling "7" products.
  • 82.1% were selling unregistered kratom products.

Those are KCAC's numbers.

Registered-market regulation was in place.

The stores sold the products anyway.

KCAC's conclusion? More enforcement is necessary.

Yes.

That is exactly the problem.

KCPA enforcement plan, pictured here with the approximate amount of equipment apparently required.

A regulatory scheme works only if somebody:

  • finds the products;
  • tests them;
  • checks the sellers;
  • checks the registrations;
  • returns to the stores;
  • issues penalties;
  • removes illegal inventory;
  • and pays for all of it.

Writing "prohibited" into a statute does not teleport inspectors into convenience stores.

Registration without enforcement is a database.

And databases do not remove products from shelves.

8. The Rat-Math "Fact Check"

And now we arrive at the masterpiece.

And now we arrive at the masterpiece.

KCAC disputes claims about how much 7-OH is produced when humans consume mitragynine.

First, the paper says the amount cannot be calculated with certainty because an important human measurement is unknown.

Fair enough.

Then KCAC says only animal data are available for that missing measurement.

Still fair.

Then it takes the value from a rat, scales it to a 70-kilogram human and calculates an estimated human amount.

Then comes my favorite part.

KCAC acknowledges:

"It is best not to identify a specific amount" because the assumed value is uncertain.

And then identifies a specific range anyway:

1.8 to 4.6 mg.
Principal investigator, apparently.

You do not need a PharmD. You do not need to know pharmacokinetics. You barely need long division.

If the human number is unknown, borrowing a number from a rat does not suddenly make it a measured human number.

"We don't know the human value, so we used the rat value" is an estimate.

Calling the resulting number reassuring does not upgrade it.

It is still an estimate.

KCAC is entitled to argue that critics should not describe the observed blood ratio as a literal 20% dose conversion.

But KCAC cannot simultaneously say the exact human amount is unknowable and then pretend its own rat-based estimate settles the matter.

The Pattern Is the Story

KCAC's individual recommendations are not all unreasonable.

  • Age restrictions may reduce pediatric access.
  • Labels are better than mystery baggies.
  • Testing is better than contamination.
  • Registration is better than having no idea what is being sold.

The problem is what happens when KCAC asks lawmakers to treat those proposals as proof that the underlying product belongs on the market.

Read all seven papers together.

KCAC itself describes a product category that may:

  • cause dependence;
  • interact with medications;
  • contain heavy metals;
  • produce pediatric adverse events;
  • be sold without adequate labels;
  • be sold without reliable dosing tools;
  • be sold despite registration requirements;
  • require laboratory testing;
  • require age restrictions;
  • require product registration;
  • require vendor registration;
  • require enforcement;
  • and require restrictions on how it can even be administered.

Then the conclusion is:

See? We can regulate this.

Maybe.

But legislators are allowed to ask another question:

Why is government being asked to construct an elaborate safety system around a product whose defenders are simultaneously telling us that major safety and efficacy questions still require more research?

That question does not require pharmacology.

It requires common sense.

Final Open Truth

The KCAC papers look impressive.

That is their strongest feature.

The green banners look official. The references look academic. The technical vocabulary makes ordinary readers feel like they probably should not question the conclusions.

Question them anyway.

Because once the jargon is removed, the recurring argument is remarkably simple:

  • The problems are real.
  • The science is incomplete.
  • The enforcement is inadequate.
  • The marketplace is messy.
  • But please don't ban the product.
That is not a scientific conclusion.

That is the position.

And there is a difference.
